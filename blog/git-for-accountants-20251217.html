Git for Accountants

People often say that rebasing rewrites history, and for teams with an accounting mindset that immediately sounds dangerous. Accounting records are sacred; you don’t go back and edit the ledger. End of discussion.

The problem is that this analogy is applied at the wrong level.

Feature branches are not accounting records. They are working papers.

Once you look at Git through that lens, rebasing stops looking reckless and starts looking like basic record hygiene.

The Setup: We Are Accountants

Imagine we are a team of private‑equity accountants. Our job is to structure complex transactions that move money across jurisdictions — Cyprus, Panama, the UK, the Bahamas, the US. (All examples are fictional.) If you want a real‑world flavor of how complex this can get, feel free to play with this tool: TreatyHopper

Designing these transactions (and deals) is non‑trivial. We experiment with routes, intermediate accounts, and structures to minimize tax impact and risk. This work happens before anything is officially posted.

While working on a deal, we never throw paper away. Every idea goes into a tray. If an approach doesn’t work, we don’t erase it — we add a new sheet explaining why we changed course. Over time, every deal looks like a thick stack of paper that captures the entire thought process.

Now map that to Git:

  • Main branch → the posted, authoritative general ledger
  • Feature branches → working papers used to structure transactions
  • Commits on feature branches → drafts, calculations, intermediate ideas

The key rule of accounting still holds:

Once something is posted to the general ledger, it is never rewritten.

The real decision we must make is organizational:

Which papers belong in the official ledger, and which remain as working papers?

Meet Our Office Assistant: Git

Enter our office assistant. Her name is Git.

She is extremely fast and very experienced. She has worked at startups and at large, highly regulated organizations. One of her defining traits is that she is very literal: she will do exactly what you ask her to do.

If you tell her to pour gasoline on the general ledger and burn it, she will make you confirm the command — but she will almost never refuse.

Git is very flexible and can live with a lot of mess. In practice, though, the industry has converged on two main ways of organizing the office:

  • Rebase with squash (further I'll simply call it rebase)
  • Merge

Office That Uses Rebase: The Beautiful Thick Ledger

In a rebase‑based office, Git organizes everything around a single, authoritative ledger.

  • There is one main desk with a big, thick general ledger on top
  • Each accountant or small team has their own desk where they work on deals
  • Working papers stay on their desks (feature branches) until the deal is finalized

When a deal is ready:

  • Git takes the finalized transaction sheets
  • She appends them to the end of the current ledger
  • The ledger remains strictly chronological

The room looks almost like a classroom: one teacher’s desk with the official ledger, and many smaller desks where work happens in parallel.

Office That Uses Merge: Registry and File Cabinets

In a merge‑based office, the layout is different.

Git keeps:

  • A small registry on the main desk
  • A large set of file cabinets, full of folders

Each folder represents a branch and contains everything:

  • Drafts
  • Detours
  • Abandoned ideas
  • Back‑and‑forth discussions

When a deal is finished:

  • The folder is stored in the cabinet
  • A note is added to the registry saying that this folder was reconciled with the ledger

Git keeps track of everything — and she means everything.

For the accountants, day‑to‑day work feels similar in both setups: take a copy of the ledger, design the deal, and give Git the result. The difference is how the result is filed.

The Problem: Reconciliation

Eventually, a problem appears.

The chief accountant finds a discrepancy and wants to reconcile the last ten additions. What matters most is when the discrepancy was introduced.

Reconciliation with a Thick Ledger (Rebase)

A chronologically ordered ledger is a huge advantage.

  • Find the most recent known‑good state
  • Check halfway toward the end of the book
  • If the numbers don’t match, repeat the process halfway again

This quickly narrows the search to the exact transaction that introduced the discrepancy.

Git has a name for this process: bisect.

Reconciliation with a Registry and File Cabinets (Merge)

Git can apply the same strategy, but the work is different.

Instead of walking backward through a single book, she must:

  • Consult the registry
  • Pull a folder from the cabinet
  • Ask you whether the state is correct
  • Put the folder back and pull another

Eventually, she will present you with a folder that contains the problem.

The Crucial Difference

Here is the key trade‑off:

  • With rebase, you learn exactly when the discrepancy was introduced
  • With merge, you learn everything about how the deal evolved

Rebase optimizes for understanding the outcome.

Merge optimizes for preserving the entire journey — memos, false starts, discussions, and abandoned ideas.

Neither approach is wrong. They answer different questions.

Fast Forward Back

When deciding how to organize your office, choose deliberately.

  • With a small team and few parallel deals, a merge‑based setup may naturally resemble a chronological ledger
  • With many teams and many concurrent deals, the registry and file cabinets can grow very complex

Both approaches serve a purpose:

  • Rebase (often with squash) produces a clean, chronological ledger that is easy to audit and reconcile
  • Always‑merge produces a rich archive that preserves every detail of how decisions were made

The right choice depends on what you value more:

  • Fast, reliable reconciliation
  • Or deep historical context

Choose the structure you want to reason about later.
